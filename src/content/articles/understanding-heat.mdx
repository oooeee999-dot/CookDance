Great cooking is often less about following a timer and more about noticing what is happening in front of you. Heat changes texture, drives off moisture, and creates new aromas. Learning to read those changes makes a recipe more useful—and makes you less dependent on one.

## Start with the sound

When food touches a hot pan, listen. A steady sizzle suggests moisture is leaving the surface. If the sound disappears and ingredients sit in a pool of liquid, the pan may be crowded. Cook in smaller batches when browning matters.

## Give the pan time

Preheating helps food cook more evenly. Add oil once the pan is warm, then add ingredients. The right setting depends on the pan, the food, and the heat source, so watch for visual cues rather than treating a dial number as universal.

## Choose the result you want

Higher heat can encourage browning when the surface is relatively dry. Gentler heat gives you more control for delicate foods and longer cooking. Neither is inherently better. The useful question is: what texture and flavor are you trying to build?

## A simple habit to practice

Next time you sauté vegetables, notice the sound, steam, and color for the first few minutes. Adjust the heat when those cues change. This is a practice prompt, not a report of a CookDance-tested experiment.
